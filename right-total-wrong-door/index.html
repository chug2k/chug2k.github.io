Send the lunch trays?

At a print fair, the Ink table ordered 17 lunch packs and the Fold table ordered 18. After packing, six people move from Fold to Ink. Each person gets one pack.

Ink needs 23. It started with 17 people and gained six.

Fold needs 12. It started with 18 and lost six.

Move six closed packs from Fold to Ink. Both 17 + 18 and 23 + 12 equal 35.

Watch six lunches change trays

The people move first. Before the runner moves a pack, predict how many lunches each table needs.

Ink starts with 17 packs and Fold with 18. After six people switch from Fold to Ink, six packs follow. Ink now has 23, Fold has 12, and the runner still carries 35 lunches.

A test for your next handoff

Take a passing sheet. Move one unit from one nonempty group to another. The total must still pass, and the two group checks must now fail. Restore the counts and reorder the rows. Everything should pass again.

Try it with two shelves that need five and eight books. Shelving four and nine keeps all 13 books in the room. Which shelf is short?

Instructor key & timing

Run it in pairs. Give learners two minutes to call the lunch slip, four to repair a film export and two to design a check. Ask what a totals-only test would miss.

Lunch. Hold the trays. Ink needs 23 and Fold needs 12. Sending the original slip leaves Ink six short and Fold six over.

Film. On sheet A, the corrected export reads DRF 7 s, ARR 11 s and ORB 18 s. Both cuts run for 36 s. Sorting the quantities also gives the same list. A cue can carry the wrong value even when every value appears once.

Debrief. Match the IDs and units. Flag a missing, extra or repeated ID. Then subtract expected from actual for each ID. Every difference must be zero. On the shelves, the first is one short and the second one over.
