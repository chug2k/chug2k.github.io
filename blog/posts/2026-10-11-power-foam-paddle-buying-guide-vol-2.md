In June, the question was which foam paddle hit hardest without costing an organ. In October, the better question is what the damn thing does after you've played with it for a while.

The cheap paddles got much more interesting. Some expensive surfaces aged badly in follow-up testing. Some supposedly durable surfaces disagreed with their own launch reviews. And “tournament approved” still requires you to finish the sentence.

This is Vol. 2, researched through October 11. [Vol. 1, from June 4, is still here](/paddle-buying-guide-2026/). Start around $100. Spend more for a specific feel, shape, or better-supported surface life. Your shopping cart does not need a seventh generation of anything.

# The summer changed the buying question

The useful advance is a surface that keeps doing its job. Launch-day spin is easy to get excited about. Launch-day spin that leaves before the credit-card statement arrives is less charming.

The best follow-up I found is [John Kew's September 4 report, starting at 56:23](https://youtu.be/G20x87p4k-0?t=3383). His own Ultré Power 2 went from **2,384 to 2,317 RPM after 100 games**: about **2.8% less spin**. That was one paddle, 24 sessions, overwhelmingly indoors. It's a useful real-play result, not proof that every HexGrit paddle lasts forever outdoors in Saigon.

The other useful advance is affordability. Pickleball Effect's [October comparison](https://pickleballeffect.com/equipment-reviews/best-100-dollar-power-pickleball-paddles/) puts the V-Sol Tour, Quanta Pro and Spitfire in the same roughly $100 shopping trip, with different power and impact-feel profiles. That makes “you need a $300 paddle to get serious” a much harder argument to sell.

Also: stop treating June's spreadsheets as timeless decimal-point truth. Kew [changed his Power and Pop methodology in late August](https://johnkewpickleball.beehiiv.com/p/power-and-pop-metrics-are-getting-an-overhaul), moving from human shots and radar toward a model based on controlled cannon measurements and paddle properties. Don't subtract an old score from a new score and announce that a paddle gained horsepower. His KewCOR is an independent test metric, not a tournament certificate.

# Around $100: buy the feel, read the asterisk

## Spartus Spitfire Elongated: my budget starting point

The [Spitfire is $109.99](https://gospartus.com/products/spitfire-elongated), with a 16mm foam core, 16.5-by-7.5-inch face and a 5.5-inch handle. Spartus lists a 180-day limited warranty and warns that fulfillment can take up to seven additional business days. Available to order does not mean arriving tomorrow.

Pickleball Effect calls it the most approachable of its three budget power options, with a softer, hollow impact. Another useful perspective is [Sid Parfait's September 11 review](https://www.pickletip.com/spartus-spitfire-review/): roughly 18 hours of play, emphasizing stronger full swings with a calmer response on short strokes. He also uses an affiliate code. This is a second person's court experience, not a second laboratory.

**Buy it for that combination and price. Don't buy it because “PermaGritNano” sounds legally incapable of wearing out.** In [Kew's July testing](https://johnkewpickleball.beehiiv.com/p/johnkew-newsletter-joola-s-new-patent-grab-the-pakle-fuse-verdict), its spin fell 14.7% after accelerated impacts, placing it in his Tier 3. Pickleball Effect's different abrasion test found strong texture retention. Both results can be real. Sanding a surface and repeatedly striking it aren't the same experiment.

There is also an [October 8 owner report of lost bite after about 50 hours](https://www.reddit.com/r/Pickleball/comments/1x0gw1u/spartus_spitfire_lost_most_of_its_bite_after_50/). That's an anecdote, with no controlled before-and-after measurement. It belongs in the uncertainty column, not in a headline declaring a product failure epidemic.

## Vatic V-Sol Tour and Ronbus Quanta Pro: two different alternatives

The [Vatic V-Sol Tour is $109.99](https://vaticpro.com/products/v-sol-tour), with a 16mm floating EPP core, EVA perimeter and a one-year warranty. The [Ronbus Quanta Pro R3.16 is $119.99](https://ronbus.com/collections/quanta-series/products/quanta-pro-r3-16), listed in stock with a six-month manufacturing-defect warranty. Pickleball Effect advertises $99.99 for either with its PBEFFECT code.

In that reviewer's comparison, **Vatic is the firmer, denser and more offensive option; Quanta Pro is the stiff, hollow middle ground; Spitfire is the softer option.** Those differences are more useful than inventing a universal winner. Quanta Pro also comes in R2 widebody and R4 hybrid shapes, so the R3 isn't compulsory if you don't want an elongated paddle.

Vatic needs the larger asterisk. Pickleball Studio published [“The Vatic Pro V-Sol Tour has a Grit Problem” on September 14](https://youtu.be/oxvImu6Oo0A). On September 15, [Kew's retest description](https://youtu.be/57m00Y6lt-w) reported a second production sample losing **15% of spin**, versus the earlier, caveated Tier 1 result. It explicitly says this does not establish how every production paddle performs. Proposed curing explanations remain hypotheses.

My call: Vatic is still a plausible value purchase for someone who likes its feel. I would not pay specifically for a promise of reliably elite grit durability until production consistency is better established. A launch review and a retest aren't two votes to average. The retest changes what you know.

# $160–$220: spend for the thing you actually need

## Jelly Bean 2: for people whose problem is already too much pop

The [Jelly Bean 2 costs $169.99](https://11six24.com/collections/frontpage/products/ultre-jelly-bean-2), uses a softer EMA foam core and HexGrit, and comes in four shapes. The Ultré page showed stock and a 12-month manufacturing-defect warranty. Normal wear is excluded. It is a different proposition from the old budget Jelly Bean; the shared candy name is doing a lot of work.

In [Pickleball Effect's September 22 hybrid comparison](https://pickleballeffect.com/equipment-reviews/volair-cmd-hybrid-vs-11six24-jelly-bean-2-ultre-vs-power-2-ultre/), Jelly Bean 2 Ultré is the softest, most controlled option, Power 2 Ultré the most offensive, and Volair CMD closer to the offensive end than its soft feel suggests.

That's why the Jelly Bean gets my touch recommendation. If you can already generate pace but your blocks keep becoming chest-high gifts, adding more trampoline isn't a development plan. The Pegasus is the widebody option; the Ultré trades some width for reach. Don't apply an Ultré test number to the Pegasus and pretend you measured both.

## Ultré Power 2: the stronger evidence pick for a power player

The [Ultré Power 2 lists at $209.99](https://11six24.com/pages/ultre-power-2-product), with a 16mm core and HexGrit face. Its square-topped hybrid outline gives a different shape option from the familiar rounded paddle tops. The manufacturer advertises a 30-day play test, with return shipping deducted.

This is where the September 100-game result earns its keep. For a buyer who wants power and is tired of replacing a face that has lost its bite, that's a concrete reason to put this above another expensive launch with only a fresh-paddle review.

The tradeoff is the lively, firm response. Pickleball Effect's comparison puts this ahead of Jelly Bean 2 and CMD for offense. If you hate that feeling, do not let a good durability test bully you into buying the wrong paddle.

## Honolulu J2CR Crystal Blue versus Six Zero Coral Pro

Two June ideas survive, with more specificity: Honolulu still deserves a power-player's attention, and Six Zero remains worth trying for a softer response. Buy the actual surface and shape that were tested.

The [J2CR Crystal Blue is $195](https://808pickle.com/products/j2cr-crystal-blue-endurance-surface%E2%84%A2-pre-order), with standard and extended handle choices. The [Coral Pro is $220 on Six Zero's US store](https://us.sixzeropickleball.com/products/coral-pro), in hybrid, widebody and elongated forms. Both are 16mm models; neither name is a standardized “generation” of performance.

[Kew's August comparison of the J2CR Long Handle Blue Grit and Coral Pro Hybrid](https://www.johnkewpickleball.com/honolulu-j2cr-blue-grit-vs-six-zero-coral-pro-hybrid) found similar power and Tier 1 spin retention, but different feel. The Honolulu was quicker and crisper; the Coral Pro was softer and more stable on off-center hits.

**Choose J2CR for fast hands, a long two-handed-backhand handle and a lively response. Choose Coral Pro Hybrid for a more muted impact and stability.** Want maximum width? Demo a widebody. Want reach? Compare elongated versions separately: that same review says the forgiveness relationship changes between the elongated shapes. “Honolulu versus Six Zero” is too broad to be a useful specification.

The Coral Pro is not automatically a low-power control paddle just because it feels plush. If taking pace out of blocks is the main goal, the Jelly Bean comparison is the more relevant starting point.

## Boomstik: no more automatic crown

June's “literal best” language was too confident. I'm retiring it. A player's response to a paddle matters, and the list of cheaper alternatives has improved. [Boomstik still lists at $333](https://www.selkirk.com/products/selkirk-labs-project-boomstik); that premium needs a personal reason, not a crown from my old article.

There is also a durability correction. In [Kew's August follow-up](https://johnkewpickleball.beehiiv.com/p/johnkew-newsletter-76-of-your-spin-loss-lands-in-the-first-50-hits-first-look-at-the-jelly-bean-2), an untouched OMNI face with InfiniGrit lost 20.8% of spin after 100 accelerated impacts. Earlier tests had started after some wear. A separate used Boomstik check showed about 12% loss after roughly 50 games, with noisy readings. **The OMNI result isn't a fresh Boomstik test.** It is enough to make a blanket premium-durability assumption look silly.

If your Boomstik still gives you predictable shots and you love it, keep it. Nothing in this research says to throw away a paddle that works.

## Loco: cheaper, better warranty wording, awkward sale conditions

The [original Loco Hybrid now lists at $149.25, down from $199](https://www.bnbpickleball.com/collections/loco/products/loco-16mm-pickleball-paddle-hybrid). That's a meaningful price change from June. But the checked Azul hybrid was backordered, and the page explicitly excludes this sale from its 30-day returns.

The current page also states a **one-year manufacturing-defect warranty** for qualifying purchases. June's six-month warning is stale for those purchases; this isn't a claim that earlier orders receive retroactive coverage.

I would consider the discounted original if you've already tried a Loco and like it. Buying a paddle you've never hit under a no-return sale is a weird way to save money. Its raw-carbon surface also lost substantial spin in Kew's August wear test. Cheap upfront and cheap over a year are different calculations.

## Friday Aura: a June recommendation, not a newly verified October winner

June favored Aura for value. This round did not produce enough new long-term evidence to renew the “best value, full stop” award. Spitfire, Quanta Pro and V-Sol Tour now make that price tier a comparison, not a coronation.

That's a limit of this research, not evidence that Aura suddenly got bad. Happy owners can happily ignore the shopping section.

## JOOLA: the interesting new question is Power FX

The [Power FX launched September 30](https://joola.com/blogs/blog/joola-introduces-power-fx-built-to-provide-effortless-power-for-every-player). Its [official product page](https://joola.com/products/perseus-power-fx-pickleball-paddle) showed **$179.95**, stock, one 16mm Perseus shape and a 12-month warranty requiring registration. It is JOOLA's new full-foam offering; Pro V's marketing generation doesn't make it the same construction.

[Kew's September 30 first-hits review](https://www.johnkewpickleball.com/joola-power-fx-review) measured 15% spin loss in accelerated wear and described an unusually stiff, high-pitched impact with noticeable off-center vibration. His court verdict was explicitly preliminary.

My recommendation is **demo it**, especially if you already like a firm paddle. A cheaper route into JOOLA's foam lineup is interesting. Eleven days after launch is not a long-term durability report, and the surface result doesn't displace the better-supported grit options above.

# Durable face. Durable core. Different questions.

A paddle can keep its texture and still develop a structural problem. A paddle can remain structurally intact while its face loses spin. “Foam is durable” doesn't resolve either question.

There is direct evidence of individual failures. In [SupaSenpai's June 18 Power 2 Hurache-X teardown](https://youtu.be/31VZmCarv1w?t=35), he describes an early face-to-core bond failure and a warranty replacement, comparing the sound before cutting the paddle open. I read the captions; I did not independently inspect the paddle. His [accompanying report](https://www.reddit.com/r/Pickleball/comments/1u95tuu/we_cut_a_disbonded_pickleball_paddle_in_half/) relays an adhesive explanation. It establishes an incident. Its speculative failure percentages are not usable population data.

Break-in needs the same discipline. You adapting to a new launch angle, a changing surface, and a failing bond are different things. I didn't find a controlled June-to-October study establishing how long all these cores last or a reliable failure-rate comparison across brands. The September HexGrit follow-up is encouraging surface evidence, not a core-lifespan certification.

If a paddle develops a bubble, a new dead area, or a sudden response change, document it and contact the seller. Don't let “it just needs breaking in” consume the return window. And read the warranty: coverage for a manufacturing defect is not a subscription to fresh grit.

For arm comfort, I'd start a demo with the softer-feeling options and a manageable swing weight, then judge actual comfort through a full session. A soft sound isn't medical evidence. This research cannot establish that any of these paddles prevents or treats elbow pain. Buying the loudest power paddle and adding a small plumbing supply of perimeter weight is not my first experiment.

# “Approved” by whom, and which paddle?

The two lists to check are [USA Pickleball's equipment registry](https://equipment.usapickleball.org/paddle-list/) and [UPA-A's approved paddles](https://upaa.unitedpickleball.com/approved-paddles/). Your event's requirements decide which matters. An approval date is also not a release date; manufacturers can certify a paddle before you can buy it.

There is an actual October change: [USA Pickleball's announcement](https://usapickleball.org/usap-news/equipment-news/update-regarding-the-usa-pickleball-spin-rate-test-and-implementation-timeline/) says new submissions from October 1 must pass a direct spin test at 2,100 RPM or below. Roughness and friction become measurements of record instead of the former upper-threshold tests. Existing certified paddles remain subject to the standards applicable when certified. A reviewer's RPM result uses its own protocol; it is not a failed official test.

On October 11, I could verify these specific records:

- **Coral Pro:** [USA Pickleball entry 13780](https://equipment.usapickleball.org/paddle-list/entry/13780/) says Pass, added October 1, model RCD 16MM, hybrid shape, diamond-infused carbon face, Peel Ply finish. UPA-A separately lists Coral Pro RCD, RCD-E and RCD-W. Six Zero's store still describes an Ultra Rough UPA-approved product. I cannot establish from those pages alone that every retail shape or version matches the new USAP entry. Check the exact model with the seller and event.
- **V-Sol Tour Elongated 16mm:** [USA Pickleball entry 13672](https://equipment.usapickleball.org/paddle-list/entry/13672/) says Pass, added August 6. That establishes this model, not every similarly named Vatic.
- **Jelly Bean 2, Ultré Power 2 and CMD HYB:** exact entries appear on UPA-A's list. I did not establish USA Pickleball approval for those models during this check.
- **Spitfire and Quanta Pro:** their manufacturers state USAP approval. The main USAP search page wasn't accessible to my research tools, so those individual official records were not independently rechecked here. Power FX's manufacturer states both approvals; its UPA-A listing was accessible.

That is a little more annoying than a green checkmark beside “legal.” It is also more useful.

# Who should actually upgrade?

**Keep your paddle** if you like its response, it remains consistent, and it meets your event's rules. Buying the newest face because a reviewer got 100 more RPM is an expensive substitute for learning your current one.

**Demo a different paddle** if you can name the problem: too much pop on blocks, sluggish hands, not enough handle for a two-hander, repeated off-center mishits, or a face that no longer gives you the spin you rely on. Match the purchase to that complaint. Lower pop, different weight distribution, more width and better-supported surface retention are concrete changes.

**Wait** if you bought a good paddle in June and your only new symptom is watching paddle YouTube. The symptoms are common. The treatment does not necessarily involve another box.

# How this was researched

This update used current manufacturer pages, official approval records, original reviewers' written testing and YouTube metadata. Auto-captions were successfully retrieved and relevant passages read for Kew's September 4 episode and SupaSenpai's June 18 teardown. Other requested captions were rate-limited or unavailable; where only a video's description or chapter list was accessible, that's the limit of the claim here. The freshest accessible detailed written evidence includes October 5–6 updates; the October 7 Studio episode is a discovery lead, not a secretly watched review.

John Kew, Pickleball Effect, Pickleball Studio, PickleTip and SupaSenpai use affiliate links or codes. Some receive review samples. Declaring editorial independence doesn't make those incentives disappear; measured methods, retests, specific samples and conflicting results get more weight than enthusiasm. Owner reports are useful leads, not failure-rate estimates. Accelerated wear results do not translate neatly into calendar months.

The June guide and [old meta-ranking](/v/best-paddles-2026-meta-ranking/) are historical context. Their claimed review totals were not re-audited or carried forward as this update's research count. The [source ledger](/research/2026-10-11-power-foam-vol-2.md) records dates, video timestamps, product-page checks and access limits. No hands-on testing by me is implied.
